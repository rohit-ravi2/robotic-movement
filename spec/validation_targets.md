## Read this first: how jump height is defined

There are two definitions in use, and they differ by roughly 5-8 cm for an SJ:

- **H_stand (apex minus standing):** height of the CM at the apex minus its height when standing upright with heels down. Used by the Bobbert / van Soest / van Ingen Schenau lab, which is also the convention of the classic simulation papers. Computed from motion capture (CM positions).
- **H_flight (apex minus takeoff):** rise of the CM after takeoff, h = v_to² / 2g. This is what force-plate impulse-momentum ("takeoff velocity") and flight-time methods give. Linthorne 2001 (S8): "The flight height is slightly less than the rise relative to the height of the c.m. when standing upright because the jumper has the ankles extended at the instant of takeoff."
- The gap is zCM at takeoff relative to standing. It is **reported directly** in two Bobbert-lab SJ datasets: 0.08 ± 0.04 m (S3, preferred posture) and 0.046 ± 0.019 m (S4, two-leg SJ).
- Flight-time methods add a further bias. Linthorne 2001: with hands on hips, CM at landing is "usually about 1-4 cm lower than at takeoff and so the flight time method overestimates the true flight height by 0.5-2 cm". Ruf et al. 2024 (S9, abstract) measured impulse-momentum vs contact-mat flight-time differences of 2.7 cm (SJ) and 3.2 cm (CMJ).

A planar torque-driven model that reports apex CM minus standing CM should be compared with H_stand targets (S1/S2, S3, S4). A model that reports v_to²/2g should be compared with H_flight targets (S5, S6, S7).

### Proposed mechanisms and what the sources say

1. **Active state / time to build force (favoured by the simulation literature).** S1 abstract: "storage and reutilization of elastic energy could be ruled out ... The crucial contribution of the countermovement seemed to be that it allowed the muscles to build up a high level of active state (fraction of attached cross-bridges) and force before the start of shortening." S2 (simulation, 4 segments and 6 muscles): the model's CMJ - SJ difference ranged "from 0.4 cm at infinitely high dSTIM/dt to about 2.5 cm at the lowest dSTIM/dt investigated". The gain came from hip extensor work over the first 30% of shortening. A model with instantaneous activation therefore gives almost no CMJ advantage. **This matters directly for a torque-driven model: without torque/activation rise-time dynamics, its CMJ - SJ difference should come out near zero.**
2. **Elastic energy storage.** S11 (Anderson & Pandy 1993, abstract): "all the elastic tissues delivered nearly the same amount of energy to the skeleton during a CMJ and an SJ ... elastic energy storage and utilization enhance jumping efficiency much more than overall jumping performance." According to S2, the S11 model's simulated CMJ was 1 cm *lower* than its SJ ("the height of their simulated CMJ was 1 cm less than that of their simulated SJ"). S15 (Komi & Bosco 1978, abstract) is the classic statement of the elastic-energy view. S13 (Kubo 1999, abstract): tendon stiffness "was inversely correlated with the difference in jump height between the vertical jumps performed with and without countermovement".
3. **Muscle slack uptake plus stimulation build-up (review synthesis).** S10 abstract: "the difference in performance may primarily be related to the greater uptake of muscle slack and the buildup of stimulation during the countermovement in a CMJ. Elastic energy may also have a small contribution." S10 judges stretch reflex and residual force enhancement unlikely to be primary contributors.
4. **Suboptimal SJ coordination: ruled out experimentally in S1.** "there were no signs of movement disintegration in SJ, and toe-off position was the same in SJ as in CMJ."
5. **Population variation.** S12 (n = 770, abstract only): the groups with the best SJ and CMJ did not show the largest difference, and males had a slightly larger difference than females. S12 and S6 come from the same laboratory and may overlap in participants, so do not count them as independent.

## The 34.6 ± 4.4 cm question

**A paper with this value does exist:** Kyne & Cronin 2025, *Sports* 13(11):379 (S5; PMID 41295762). I confirmed it in both the PubMed abstract and Table 1 of the PMC full text. Its context:
- n = 23 male athletes, **mean age 18.1 ± 0.7 yr**, so late adolescent rather than adult.
- Force-plate impulse-momentum, so it is **H_flight**.
- Hands on hips, about 90° knee start held for 4 s.
- Published November 2025. It is not part of the classic simulation-validation literature.

**Conflation risk:** the SD of 4.4 cm is identical to Bobbert et al. 1996's SJ SD (44.7 ± 4.4 cm, which is H_stand, n = 6). If a document attributes "34.6 ± 4.4" to Bobbert 1996 or to the classic simulation literature, that is a mis-attribution. The two values use different height definitions and differ by about 10 cm.

## What could not be verified

- **Bobbert et al. 1996 (S1) full text.** I could not read the starting knee angle, arm position, push-off duration, takeoff height or peak GRF. The absolute means (44.7 / 48.1 cm) come only through S2's citation. The 3.4 cm difference is in the S1 abstract itself.
- **Harman et al. 1990 (S14).** I did not see SJ/CMJ heights for its four arm/countermovement conditions (n = 18 males). The abstract only has qualitative statements and "Peak positive power averaged over 3,000 W". Any SJ height attributed to Harman is UNVERIFIED.
- **Komi & Bosco 1978 (S15).** No numbers in the abstract. Heights UNVERIFIED.
- **Pandy et al. 1990, Pandy & Zajac 1991, Anderson & Pandy 1993 / 1999 (S11, S16).** The experimental subject SJ/CMJ heights and the simulated heights used in these papers' validations are UNVERIFIED (abstracts only; the open-archive PDFs were blocked). Pandy & Zajac 1991 define jump height as "the net vertical displacement of the center of mass of the body from standing" (abstract), i.e. H_stand.
- **Bobbert & van Soest 1994 (S17), Domire & Challis 2007 (S18), Gheller et al. 2015 (S19), Kubo et al. 1999 (S13), Markovic et al. 2004 (S20), Kozinc et al. 2022 (S12).** I could not get their numeric SJ values (abstracts only).
- **Ruf et al. 2024 (S9).** SJ/CMJ means for 23 professional soccer players are shown only in figures. Not extracted.
- **SJ takeoff velocity.** No accessible source reports it directly. All values above are DERIVED.
- **Total-body peak GRF in BW for a Bobbert-lab SJ.** Only the per-leg value is available (S4).
- **van Ingen Schenau / Bobbert & van Ingen Schenau 1988 (PMID 3379084).** These are CMJ studies (the abstract says "Ten skilled jumpers performed jumps with preparatory countermovement"). No SJ value.
